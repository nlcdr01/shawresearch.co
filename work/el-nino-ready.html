The problem

El Niño guidance is usually written for a country or a region. The people who act on it, a city council member, a clinic, or a mutual-aid group, need to know what has happened in their place before, who is most exposed, and which few actions will save the most lives.

Writing that by hand takes an expert days per place. A volunteer team cannot do it at the rate requests arrive, and a fast answer that is wrong is worse than none.

What I builtSix steps
1Rules

Intake gate. A request arrives from the website form. The gate checks it is precise enough to act on (a neighborhood or district, not "Brazil") and decides: build, ask a question, or hold.

2AI

Local history. Research the El Niño and climate events that have hit that exact place, and what they cost.

3Rules

The numbers. Projected excess deaths come from Climate Impact Lab data, not from the model's judgment.

4AI

Ranked actions. Eight expert perspectives, including a funder's view of where money does the most good and how much it takes, and a view of how food reaches the community.

5AI

Independent check. A separate pass verifies every recommendation against its source before anything is formatted.

6Person

Approval. The reply and PDF are saved as a draft. Nothing is sent until I have read it. Every edition carries a health-wording note that limits the team's liability.

Why it transfers

The subject is climate. The method is not. Any expert deliverable that a team produces again and again (a site-survey report, a proposal, an onboarding pack, or a customer status brief) has the same shape: an intake that decides whether the request is ready, research and drafting that AI can do, numbers that should come from a named source, and a person who signs.

Take the magic out of the process: repeatable steps, each with a known cost, timeline, and owner.

That was the brief for SD-WAN at 128 Technology. With AI, the steps are cheaper to build, and the build time drops from months to days.

What stays private
