PMP + AI: the lived experience

A project manager takes in scattered pieces of information, fits them into what is already known, and uses the result to steer a bounded piece of work, continuously and in real time.

Angus Shaw, PMP · November 2026

The job

That sentence hides most of the work. So does the job title.

The information arrives in four kinds:

  • Resources. Who is available, which rooms, and which equipment.
  • Money. The budget, what the resources cost, and the price of the work.
  • Time. How long a task is taking, whether it has started, and whether it is done.
  • Assumptions. Do we have a product that works? What have we promised about it, to whom, and by when? Can we keep all of those promises?

It arrives through meetings, emails, texts, Slack, WhatsApp, and conversations in the corridor. Pulling it together usually means building something in Excel or Word.

What goes out is direction. A person or a team learns what the next action is, so the work keeps moving. When a decision is above your authority, you escalate, and a good escalation gives the person you escalate to enough information to confirm your proposed path, so they can sign it off quickly.

Each step up multiplies the inputs. The number of hours in the day stays the same. I did the portfolio version for six years, across 60 customers, from a single router to more than 10,000.

Before

You take meeting notes by hand while you juggle seven other to-dos. Almost everything lives in your head.

The documents are never finished in time to change anything. Their real job is to limit liability. The target is the end of the day; that becomes the end of the week, or just before the next meeting.

The work feels like a jigsaw puzzle with no box lid. You fit pieces together, notice what is missing, and go looking for it. Your email history is the most valuable resource you have, and your speed depends on two skills: finding things in it, and knowing who to ask the key question.

Departments often disagree about what was promised. Each disagreement becomes an internal escalation, and each escalation costs time.

A promise nobody could keep

I once ran a project to replace a large, critical network for a public-sector customer. Sales, delivery, and product each made commitments, and the gaps between those commitments shaped the project more than any technical problem did.

One gap did more damage than all the others.

Partway through, a piece of hardware at the center of the design was given an end-of-life date. Its replacement ran a newer operating system, and that system was less mature. It did not yet do everything the old one did.

Inside the company, the product team said the new platform would match the old one's features. It could not, not on the timeline we needed. Nobody outside the room heard that promise, but the whole plan rested on it.

When the gap came out, it delayed the project by years. Fixing the technical problem carried a significant cost, and so did repairing the customer relationship.

No one was careless. The information that would have stopped it existed, in a feature list, in an engineer's head, and in an email thread. It just was not in the room when the decision was made.
After

My AI assistant writes the meeting notes, and I can review them as the meeting happens. A commitment can be approved while everyone is still in the room.

Email and every messaging app feed into the same place as the notes. Together they build a status report that updates itself, and every version is kept in a log. That makes every commitment reviewable, and it shows the effect of each new commitment on every project, program, and portfolio document straight away. A scheduling conflict or a missing piece of required functionality is flagged when the commitment is made, not months or years later when it is missed.

Go back to the hardware replacement. With this setup, the gap shows up in the meeting where the replacement is proposed. The system holds what the customer was promised and what each platform can actually do. When someone says "the new one will match," the gap is on the screen before the meeting ends. It becomes a decision made with the facts, not a surprise years later. The check is only as good as what it is fed, so the feature lists for both platforms have to be in it. That is a one-off job, and the cost of skipping it was years.

When a project needs a new kind of document, it is built overnight. The method that built it is kept, so the next project gets it for free.

The only gap left is a conversation in person that nobody wrote down. Those now need to be recorded on purpose. And the outstanding work is completed or scheduled by the end of each day.
